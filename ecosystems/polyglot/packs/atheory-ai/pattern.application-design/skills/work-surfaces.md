# Choose work surfaces from task relationships

Choose the primary work surface from what people need to manipulate, compare, or understand. Supporting surfaces supply context or controls for that work. A familiar sidebar/canvas/inspector arrangement is a candidate, not a requirement.

Spatial relationships can justify a canvas; repeated attribute comparison can justify a table; sequential decisions can justify a form or step flow. A product can use different surfaces for different tasks without changing its object vocabulary. Begin with representative content and a task walkthrough rather than choosing a layout from an empty screenshot.

Decide which information must be visible together. Frequent comparison benefits from simultaneous views; occasional reference may fit a contextual panel. Give the primary task enough space before allocating permanent regions to secondary activity. Persistent navigation earns its space when switching destinations is part of the work.

## Example

A workflow's connected nodes need a spatial editor. Reviewing twenty run durations needs a table, not twenty canvas cards. Editing a selected node can use an inspector while its connections remain visible. A schedule-only tool may need a form and preview instead of a canvas.

## Verify

Each surface choice has a task-based reason and an alternative that was considered. A realistic walkthrough exposes the necessary information without repeated switching or memorization. The working area remains useful with long names, large objects, and supporting panels open. Compact presentation is a separate arrangement of the same task relationships.
